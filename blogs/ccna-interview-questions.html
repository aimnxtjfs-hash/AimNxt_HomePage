A first-round CCNA interview is shorter than people expect. Twenty-five to forty minutes, twelve to eighteen questions, and the first four of them are warm-up. By the time you reach the routing questions the interviewer has usually made up their mind, which is why the questions that feel easiest are the ones worth preparing hardest.

The thirty questions below are grouped the way the conversation actually moves: vocabulary, then switching, then routing, then services and security, then two or three scenarios that decide it. Each one carries the answer a fresher should give and, separately, what the interviewer is scoring while you give it. That second line is the part no other list publishes, and in our trainers’ experience it is the part candidates lose on.

We also did something none of the pages ranking for this search has done: we read what Hyderabad employers actually ask for. On 6 October 2026 we counted how often each of these topics appears in Hyderabad and Secunderabad job listings on Naukri. Two of those numbers should change how you prepare, and one of them is about a protocol the CCNA 200-301 syllabus does not teach you at all.

What questions are asked in a CCNA interview?

A CCNA interview opens with five or six definition questions — OSI layers, TCP against UDP, router against switch — then moves to VLANs, trunking and spanning tree, then to routing tables, static routes and OSPF, and ends with two or three troubleshooting scenarios. Expect at least one subnetting question you must work out on paper.

That sequence is not an accident. It mirrors the shape of Cisco’s own blueprint, where Network Fundamentals and Network Access are each 20% of the exam, IP Connectivity is 25%, and the rest splits between IP Services, Security Fundamentals and Automation. An interviewer who has read the exam topics will spend about a quarter of the time on routing, and so should you.

What the sequence does not tell you is the weighting of the decision. The definitions are a filter: get them wrong and the interview ends early, get them right and you earn nothing. The scenarios are the test. Everything in between is evidence-gathering.

What is the interviewer actually scoring?

Interviewers score method, not recall: whether you reach for a structured answer, name the layer or the command you would check, and admit the boundary of what you know. Two candidates can define OSPF identically and only one gets through, because one of them said which show command they would run first.

Three habits separate the two. The first is naming the layer: “that sounds like Layer 2” before you start reasoning tells the interviewer you have a map. The second is naming a command: show ip interface brief, show interfaces trunk, show ip ospf neighbor. Candidates who name commands are assumed to have typed them. The third is the honest stop. “I have read about BGP but I have not configured it” scores better than a wrong paragraph, every single time, because the interviewer’s real question is whether they can trust what you tell them on a Tuesday afternoon when something is broken.

This is also where hands-on training shows up in a transcript you cannot fake. Trainers on the AimNxt CCNA and CCNP networking course in Hyderabad run Module 6 mock interview sessions for exactly this reason: the first time you explain OSPF out loud should not be in front of a hiring manager.

CCNA interview questions on networking basics (Q1–Q8)

The first eight questions in a CCNA interview test vocabulary, and the fastest way to fail them is a long answer. Interviewers use them to calibrate, not to probe, so two clean sentences and a stop is the right length. Below are the eight that recur, with the answer that gets a nod.

1What is the difference between a router and a switch?

Layer 2 / Layer 3

A switch forwards frames inside one broadcast domain using MAC addresses and a MAC address table. A router forwards packets between different networks using IP addresses and a routing table, and it terminates the broadcast domain — broadcasts do not cross it.

Scoring: the phrase “broadcast domain”. Candidates who say it are thinking in layers; candidates who say “a router connects to the internet” are repeating a home-router advert.

2Explain the OSI model, and where do TCP and IP sit?

Fundamentals

Seven layers: physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application. IP operates at Layer 3 and TCP at Layer 4. The model matters in practice because it gives you an order to troubleshoot in, from the cable upwards.

Scoring: whether you can place a protocol rather than recite the list. Expect the follow-up “which layer would you check first on a link that is down” — the answer is Layer 1, and people who recited the list often miss it.

3TCP or UDP — what is the difference and when would you choose each?

Transport

TCP is connection-oriented: a three-way handshake, sequence numbers, acknowledgements and retransmission. UDP has none of that, so it is lighter and faster but delivers nothing reliably. Voice, video and DNS queries use UDP; web traffic, file transfer and email use TCP.

Scoring: one real example on each side. “Reliable versus unreliable” with no example reads as memorised.

4What happens in the TCP three-way handshake?

Transport

SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. The client sends SYN with its initial sequence number; the server replies SYN-ACK, acknowledging that number and proposing its own; the client acknowledges the server’s number. Only then does data flow.

Scoring: mentioning sequence numbers. Naming three flags is the memorised answer; explaining what each side is agreeing to is the understood one.

5What is a MAC address, and how does a switch learn one?

Switching

A 48-bit Layer 2 hardware address burned into the interface. The switch reads the source MAC of every frame arriving on a port and records that pairing in its MAC address table, ageing entries out after inactivity — 300 seconds by default on Cisco switches. A frame for an unknown destination is flooded out of every other port in the VLAN.

Scoring: the word “flooded”. It shows you know what a switch does when it does not know, which is the behaviour behind half of all Layer 2 faults.

6Collision domain or broadcast domain — what is the difference?

Fundamentals

A collision domain is a segment where two frames can collide; on a modern switch every port is its own collision domain, which is why collisions are rare. A broadcast domain is the set of devices that receive each other’s broadcasts, and it is bounded by a VLAN or a router interface.

Scoring: whether you connect it to why VLANs exist. The follow-up is usually “so how do you make more broadcast domains without buying routers?”

7What is ARP, and what do you see when it fails?

Fundamentals

ARP resolves a known IP address to an unknown MAC address inside one broadcast domain, using a broadcast request and a unicast reply. When it fails you get the classic symptom: the interface is up, the addressing looks right, and nothing passes. Look at the VLAN, a duplicate IP, or port security dropping the frame.

Scoring: describing a symptom. Anyone can define ARP; a candidate who knows what a failure looks like has watched one.

8What is a default gateway, and what happens to a host without one?

Fundamentals

The router interface a host sends any traffic to when the destination is outside its own subnet. A host with no gateway, or the wrong gateway, still reaches everything on its local subnet and fails for everything beyond it. That symptom pair identifies the fault on its own.

Scoring: giving the symptom pair unprompted — local works, remote fails. It is the fastest diagnosis in networking and interviewers listen for it.

CCNA interview questions on switching and VLANs (Q9–Q14)

Switching questions carry real weight because VLAN appears in 979 Hyderabad listings and spanning tree protocol in 498, so an interviewer expects configuration-level answers, not definitions. Six questions cover VLANs across switches, access against trunk ports, the native VLAN, 802.1Q tagging, EtherChannel with LACP and Rapid PVST+ root election.

9What is a VLAN and why would you use one?

Switching

A VLAN is a logically separate broadcast domain created on shared switch hardware. You use one to segment traffic by function or department, to limit the reach of broadcasts, and to apply security policy at the routed boundary — all without buying more switches.

Scoring: whether you mention policy. “To separate departments” is half an answer; “so I can put an access list between them” is the whole one.

10Access port or trunk port — what is the difference?

Configuration

An access port belongs to exactly one VLAN and sends frames untagged to an endpoint such as a PC or phone. A trunk port carries many VLANs between switches, tagging each frame with its VLAN ID using 802.1Q so the far end can separate them again.

Scoring: saying “untagged” and “802.1Q”. The likely follow-up is which command you would run to check: show interfaces trunk.

11What is the native VLAN, and why does a mismatch matter?

Trap question

The native VLAN is the one VLAN carried untagged across a trunk, VLAN 1 by default. If the two ends of a trunk disagree about it, traffic from one VLAN silently arrives in another — two broadcast domains merge, nothing logs an error, and the fault is invisible in a ping test.

Scoring: the word “silently”. This is a deliberate trap: the candidate who only memorised the definition says “VLAN 1” and stops.

12What is spanning tree, and what problem does it solve?

Switching

Spanning tree stops Layer 2 loops by blocking redundant paths while keeping them available as backups. It elects a root bridge by lowest bridge ID — priority first, then MAC address — and the CCNA 200-301 v1.1 blueprint tests Rapid PVST+, including root election and PortFast.

Scoring: naming the tie-break order. “Lowest MAC” alone is incomplete, because priority is what engineers actually change.

13What is EtherChannel, and which protocol negotiates it?

Configuration

EtherChannel bundles up to eight physical links into one logical link for more bandwidth and for redundancy without spanning tree blocking anything. LACP, defined in 802.3ad, is the standards-based negotiation protocol; PAgP is the Cisco one. Both ends must agree on speed, duplex and allowed VLANs or the bundle will not form.

Scoring: the matching requirement. It is the reason real EtherChannels fail, and it shows you have built one.

14What is port security, and what are its violation modes?

Security

Port security limits which MAC addresses, and how many, a switch port will accept. The three violation modes are protect, which silently drops offending frames, restrict, which drops and logs, and shutdown, which puts the port into err-disabled state. Shutdown is the default.

Scoring: knowing err-disabled recovery, either manually with shutdown then no shutdown, or automatically with errdisable recovery. That is the bit a support engineer needs on day one.

An interviewer asking about trunking and the native VLAN is picturing a rack like this one. Candidates who have stood in front of one answer differently from candidates who have not.

CCNA interview questions on IP routing (Q15–Q21)

IP Connectivity is 25% of the CCNA 200-301 v1.1 exam and roughly the same share of the interview, so seven routing questions is normal. They cover reading a routing table, longest prefix match, administrative distance, static and default routes, OSPF neighbour states, DR and BDR election, and first hop redundancy.

15How does a router choose between two routes to the same destination?

High value

Longest prefix match first: the most specific route wins regardless of anything else. Only if two routes have the same prefix length does the router compare administrative distance, and only if those tie does it compare metric. That order never changes.

Scoring: the order itself. Candidates who lead with administrative distance have learned the facts without the hierarchy, and most scenario questions turn on exactly this.

16What is administrative distance? Name three values.

Routing

Administrative distance ranks how much the router trusts a source of routing information when two sources offer the same prefix. Lower is better. Directly connected is 0, a static route is 1, EIGRP internal is 90, OSPF is 110 and RIP is 120.

Scoring: the numbers, said without hesitation. This is one of the few pure-recall questions where fluency is the signal.

17Walk me through a line in a routing table.

Interpretation

Left to right: the routing protocol code, then the prefix and mask, then administrative distance and metric in square brackets, then the next hop address, the age of the entry, and the outgoing interface. Above the table sits the gateway of last resort.

Scoring: this is the single most common whiteboard question in Indian network interviews, because it proves you have looked at real output rather than slides.

18Static route or default route — what is the difference?

Routing

A static route points at one specific prefix you configure by hand. A default route is the static route for 0.0.0.0/0, which catches everything the table does not match. A floating static route is the same destination with a deliberately higher administrative distance, so it sits unused until the primary path disappears.

Scoring: mentioning the floating static. It answers the unasked question of how you build a backup path without a routing protocol.

19What are the OSPF neighbour states, and where does it get stuck?

High value

Down, Init, 2-Way, ExStart, Exchange, Loading, Full. Stuck at 2-Way is normal between two DROther routers. Stuck at ExStart usually means an MTU mismatch. A neighbour that never appears at all normally means mismatched area, mismatched subnet, mismatched hello or dead timers, or authentication.

Scoring: the troubleshooting half. Listing the states is the easy part; naming what each stall means is what separates a studied candidate from a practised one. Command to name: show ip ospf neighbor.

20How are the OSPF DR and BDR elected?

Routing

Highest interface priority wins; if priorities tie, the highest router ID wins. The election happens only on broadcast and non-broadcast multi-access segments, not on point-to-point links, and it is non-preemptive — a better router arriving later does not take over until the current DR fails.

Scoring: “non-preemptive”, and knowing that priority 0 removes a router from the election entirely.

21What is a first hop redundancy protocol?

Routing

It lets two or more routers share one virtual gateway IP address, so hosts keep their default gateway when a router fails. HSRP is Cisco’s, VRRP is the open standard, and GLBP adds load balancing. CCNA v1.1 tests the concepts; the v2.0 topics name HSRP and VRRP explicitly.

Scoring: knowing that the hosts never change anything. Candidates who describe reconfiguring the gateway have missed the point of the protocol.

CCNA interview questions on IP services and security (Q22–Q27)

Six questions here cover NAT, DHCP, DNS, syslog severity levels, access control lists and port security — and access control lists matter most, appearing in 1,222 Hyderabad listings on 6 October 2026. NAT and DHCP appear in even more, though those counts include cloud and server roles that only mention the protocol.

22What is NAT, and how is PAT different?

Configuration

NAT rewrites addresses as traffic crosses a boundary, usually private to public. PAT, which Cisco configures as NAT overload, maps many inside addresses to one outside address by giving each session a different source port. The terms to know are inside local, inside global, outside local and outside global.

Scoring: naming the port as the thing that keeps sessions apart. That one sentence is the whole of PAT.

23Explain the DHCP process.

Services

DORA: the client broadcasts Discover, the server replies Offer, the client sends Request for the offered address, the server sends Acknowledge. Because Discover is a broadcast, a server on another subnet never hears it — which is why you configure a relay with ip helper-address on the client-side interface.

Scoring: the relay. Reciting DORA is expected; explaining why a relay exists shows you understand broadcast boundaries.

24What are the syslog severity levels?

Services

Eight levels, 0 to 7: emergency, alert, critical, error, warning, notification, informational, debugging. Lower numbers are more severe. When a monitoring team says “log level 4 and above” they mean warnings and anything worse, and debugging at level 7 is the one you must remember to switch off.

Scoring: direction. Half of all candidates get the scale backwards, so saying “lower is more severe” explicitly is worth doing.

25Standard or extended ACL — what is the difference, and where do you apply each?

High value

A standard ACL matches source address only, numbered 1–99 and 1300–1999, and is traditionally placed close to the destination. An extended ACL matches source, destination, protocol and port, numbered 100–199 and 2000–2699, and goes close to the source. Every ACL ends in an implicit deny, and entries are evaluated top down.

Scoring: the implicit deny and the top-down order. Almost every ACL scenario question is really a question about entry order.

26What are DHCP snooping and dynamic ARP inspection?

Security

DHCP snooping marks ports as trusted or untrusted and drops DHCP server messages arriving on untrusted ports, which stops a rogue DHCP server handing out addresses. Dynamic ARP inspection then uses the binding table that snooping built to drop forged ARP replies, which stops ARP spoofing.

Scoring: the dependency. Saying that DAI relies on the snooping binding table shows you understand them as one control, not two facts.

27How do you secure management access to a switch?

Configuration

SSH instead of Telnet: set a hostname and domain name, generate an RSA key, create a local user, then set transport input ssh and login local on the VTY lines. Add enable secret, an access list restricting which subnets may reach the VTYs, and an exec timeout.

Scoring: mentioning the ACL on the VTY lines. Everyone says “use SSH not Telnet”; few go on to restrict who can even attempt it.

CCNA scenario-based interview questions (Q28–Q30)

Scenario questions decide the interview, and all three follow one shape: a user cannot reach something, and you must say what you check first. There is no right answer, only a right order — physical layer, then addressing, then the path — and saying your order out loud is what scores.

28A user cannot reach a file server. Walk me through it.

Scenario

Scope it first: one user or the whole floor? Then work upward. Check link and interface status for errors and duplex or speed mismatch. Check the host’s IP, mask and gateway. Ping the gateway, then a remote IP, then the same host by name — that last step separates a DNS problem from a routing problem. Then look at the routing table and any access list in the path.

Scoring: saying what each step eliminates. A candidate who lists commands is guessing in order; a candidate who says “if the gateway pings but the remote IP does not, the problem is past my switch” is troubleshooting.

29Two switches are cabled together, but hosts in the same VLAN cannot talk. What now?

Scenario

Check the trunk, in this order: is the link actually trunking, is that VLAN in the allowed list, does the VLAN exist in the VLAN database on both switches, do the native VLANs match, and is spanning tree blocking the port. The first command is show interfaces trunk, and the second is show vlan brief on both ends.

Scoring: checking that the VLAN exists on both switches. It is the most commonly missed cause, because the cable and the trunk both look healthy.

30Someone patched in a new link and the whole network slowed down. What do you suspect?

Scenario

A Layer 2 loop. Broadcast traffic multiplies around it, switch CPUs climb, and MAC address tables churn as the same address appears on different ports. Look for a redundant link where spanning tree is disabled or where PortFast was configured on a port now facing another switch, and check interface counters for a broadcast storm.

Scoring: naming the symptom chain — broadcasts, CPU, MAC flapping. Anyone can say “loop”; explaining how a loop makes itself visible is the senior answer.

Notice what all three have in common: none of them can be answered from a definition. This is why candidates who revise two hundred questions still stall in round one, and it is also why Cisco’s next exam version leans harder on this skill, which we come to below. If you are mapping the whole journey rather than one interview, our network engineer skills roadmap sets the scenario work in order.

How much subnetting will a CCNA interview ask?

Expect one or two subnetting questions worked on paper, usually a host count converted to a mask or a given address placed in its subnet. Subnetting appeared in only 128 Hyderabad listings on 6 October 2026, the lowest count we measured, yet it is the most reliably asked CCNA interview question.

The two shapes to practise are these. First: “you need 60 hosts per subnet, what mask do you use?” — /26, because 2⁶ gives 64 addresses and 62 usable. Second: “which subnet does 192.168.10.75/27 belong to?” — blocks of 32, so 192.168.10.64 to 192.168.10.95, with 192.168.10.65 to .94 usable and .95 as the broadcast. If you can do those two in under thirty seconds without a chart, you are past the filter.

The reason this question survives. Subnetting is the only CCNA topic that cannot be faked, blagged or half-answered. The arithmetic is either right or wrong in front of the interviewer, which makes it the cheapest reliable signal they have. That is also why it keeps appearing even in interviews for roles whose job description never mentions it.

Will they ask about BGP, which CCNA does not cover?

Yes, and it is the most common unfair question in a Hyderabad network interview: BGP appeared in 1,011 listings on 6 October 2026, more than OSPF at 624, while CCNA 200-301 v1.1 does not test BGP at all. Domain 3.0 covers static routing, single-area OSPFv2 and FHRP concepts only.

So a CCNA-qualified fresher walks into an interview for a role whose advert says BGP, and gets asked a question their certification never prepared them for. Nothing on Google’s first page for this search warns you about that. The answer is not to cram BGP; it is to have one honest, well-shaped sentence ready.

What to say when BGP comes up

“BGP is the exterior gateway protocol that carries routes between autonomous systems, and it is path-vector rather than link-state. It is not on the CCNA 200-301 blueprint, so I have read it rather than configured it — I have configured static routing and single-area OSPFv2 on real routers.” That answer is accurate, shows the boundary of your experience, and redirects to what you can actually demonstrate.

The same applies to MPLS, EIGRP and multi-area OSPF, all of which show up in Hyderabad adverts and none of which CCNA tests. They belong to the professional level, which is why the Networking Ignite Programme continues into Module 2, CCNP ENCOR, where OSPF and EIGRP are taught at enterprise scale. Does CCNA 200-301 v2.0 change what interviews ask?

It changes the emphasis. Cisco published CCNA v2.0 exam topics on 20 May 2026, first testable 3 February 2027, and the word Troubleshoot appears in six of its 29 topics against zero of v1.1’s 53. Interviews already lean that way, so scenario practice matters more than definition practice.

Add “Diagnose”, which appears twice more, and eight of the 29 v2.0 topics are fault-finding — roughly 28% of the blueprint. Version 1.1 asked you to describe, explain and configure; version 2.0 asks you to fix. Cisco also renames four of the six domains, merges security into services, and promotes AI into a domain title. The exam code, the price and the 120-minute duration all stay the same. We read both official topic lists directly: the Cisco CCNA exam topics page carries the v1.1 blueprint and links the v2.0 one.

What this means for your interview preparation is simple. Every page offering you two hundred CCNA questions is built on the v1.1 definition style. From February 2027 the credential behind those questions will be a troubleshooting credential, and interviewers — who already ask scenario questions at a higher rate than the old blueprint implied — will have Cisco’s own wording to back them up. The full domain-by-domain comparison is in our CCNA 200-301 syllabus guide.

How do you answer “have you worked on real equipment?”

Name the hardware, name what you configured on it, and say honestly where you used a simulator instead. An interviewer asks this to find out whether your OSPF answer came from a textbook or a console. The AimNxt lab inventory lists three routers, three next-generation firewalls, three Layer 3 switches, two wireless LAN controllers.

The full inventory published in the Networking Ignite Programme curriculum is eleven line items: three routers, three next-generation firewalls, DNAC virtual access, three Layer 3 switches, two Layer 2 switches, two wireless LAN controllers, three wireless access points, an AAA server, fibre and Ethernet modules, patch cables and a console cable. A candidate who can say “I configured OSPF between two physical routers and a Layer 3 switch, and I have used Packet Tracer for the topologies we could not cable” is giving the interviewer something to verify.

Do not inflate this. Simulator work is legitimate preparation and interviewers do not penalise it — they penalise discovering that “hands-on experience” meant a video course. The sentence to avoid is any claim you could not defend if the interviewer handed you a console cable, which some of them in Hyderabad do. What mistakes cost freshers the CCNA interview?

Four mistakes end most fresher interviews: guessing instead of saying you do not know, answering a configuration question with a definition, quoting a command without knowing what its output means, and claiming hardware experience you only had in Packet Tracer. The fourth is the one interviewers catch fastest.

The four that actually lose offers

Guessing. One confident wrong answer makes every correct answer suspect, because the interviewer now has to verify all of them. “I have not worked with that” costs you one question; a bluff costs you the interview.
Defining when asked to configure. “How would you set up a trunk between these switches” is not answered by explaining what a trunk is. Name the mode, the encapsulation, the allowed VLANs and the verification command.
Commands without output. Naming show ip ospf neighbor earns credit only until the follow-up: what are you looking for in it? Know one specific thing you read from every command you mention.
Inflating hands-on work. Simulator practice is fine and worth saying. Describing it as real-equipment experience is the fastest way to lose an interviewer’s trust, and in Hyderabad some of them will hand you a console cable.

There is a fifth that is harder to coach: talking too long. A first-round interview has a question budget, and a candidate who takes three minutes on the OSI model gets asked eight questions instead of fifteen, which gives them fewer chances to show what they know. Answer, stop, let them follow up.
