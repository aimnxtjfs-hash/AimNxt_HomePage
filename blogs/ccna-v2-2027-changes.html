CCNA v2.0 (2027): What’s Changing in the 200-301 Exam and Should You Wait to Certify? We read both official Cisco blueprints side by side on 10 October 2026, counted the weeks left, and then checked whether a single Hyderabad job advert cares which version you hold

Cisco is refreshing the CCNA. The 200-301 v1.1 exam you can book today stops being available after 2 February 2027, and CCNA v2.0 opens for testing on 3 February 2027. Six domains become five, AI appears on a CCNA blueprint for the first time, and troubleshooting moves to the centre of the exam. That has produced a lot of noise and one very practical question: do you sit the exam you have been studying for, or wait? This article answers it with the two published blueprints, Cisco’s own dates, and the Hyderabad hiring data — not with guesswork.

A student in the Thursday evening batch asked the question everyone in the room was thinking. He had been studying since July, he had booked nothing yet, and he had just watched a video telling him the CCNA was about to change completely. Should he stop, he asked, and start again on the new syllabus?

The honest answer took about four minutes, and most of it was reading Cisco’s own pages out loud. The refresh is real. The dates are fixed. But almost nothing he had learned since July was wasted, and the version he sits will not appear on his certificate or in any job advert he applies to.

So this is that four-minute answer, written down with the sources attached. On 10 October 2026 we read Cisco’s published v1.1 blueprint and its published v2.0 blueprint side by side, noted exactly what Cisco states and what it does not, counted what the Hyderabad job market actually asks for, and then built a planner that turns the calendar into your own week count. Where a number comes from a third party rather than Cisco, we say so.

What is changing in the CCNA v2.0 exam in 2027?

Cisco’s CCNA v2.0 replaces the current 200-301 v1.1 blueprint from 3 February 2027, cutting six exam domains down to five and adding AI content for the first time. The exam code, the 120-minute format and the US$300 price all stay the same. Only the topic mix and the emphasis change.

That last sentence is the part the videos tend to skip. This is a blueprint refresh, not a new certification. The exam is still called 200-301, it is still the associate-level Cisco networking exam, and the credential it awards is still CCNA. Nobody will ever ask you which edition you sat.

What does change is what the questions look like. Cisco has reorganised the domains, raised the weight of infrastructure and switching, folded security and services together, and created a small new domain that carries both AI and network operations. Alongside that, Cisco describes the refreshed exam as leaning harder on practical assessment and troubleshooting.

Same exam number, same credential

Both versions are “Implementing and Administering Cisco Solutions (200-301 CCNA)”. Cisco does not issue a “CCNA v1.1” or a “CCNA v2.0” certificate — it issues a CCNA. The version number describes the blueprint the questions were drawn from on the day you tested, and nothing else.

When exactly does CCNA v2.0 start and when does v1.1 end?

Cisco states that 2 February 2027 is the last day to test the 200-301 CCNA v1.1 exam and 3 February 2027 is the first date to test v2.0. There is no overlap window and no extension. From 10 October 2026 that leaves roughly sixteen weeks of v1.1 testing.

Those sixteen weeks are the only real constraint in this entire discussion, so it is worth seeing them laid out rather than described.

One practical detail that catches people out: Cisco charges full price per attempt, and there is a mandatory wait between attempts. If your plan depends on passing on the second try before 2 February, you need to sit the first attempt well before Christmas, not in late January. What are the five CCNA v2.0 exam domains and their weights?

The CCNA v2.0 blueprint has five domains: Network Infrastructure and Connectivity at 25%, Switching and Network Access at 25%, IP Routing at 20%, Network Services and Security at 20%, and AI, and Network Operations and Management at 10%. Cisco publishes those weights on its official v2.0 exam topics page.

Cisco also lists, in prose, the six areas the exam covers: IP routing, switching and network access, network services and security, network infrastructure and connectivity, AI, and network operations and management. The exam itself stays 120 minutes, priced at US$300, available in English and Japanese, with results typically online within 48 hours.

CCNA v2.0 domain (official)WeightWhat sits here in practice
1.0 Network Infrastructure and Connectivity25%The fundamentals layer: components, topologies, cabling, IPv4 and IPv6 addressing, connectivity verification.
2.0 Switching and Network Access25%VLANs, trunking, spanning tree, EtherChannel, wireless access and WLC configuration.

Cisco’s summary page publishes the five domain titles and the five weights; the objective-by-objective detail sits in the downloadable exam topics PDF linked from that page. Read the PDF before you buy any course that claims to be v2.0-aligned, and compare it against what you are being sold.

How do the CCNA v1.1 and v2.0 blueprints compare?

Reading the two published blueprints side by side, no domain disappears outright — they are renamed, reweighted and merged. IP Services and Security Fundamentals, 10% and 15% in v1.1, become a single 20% Network Services and Security domain. Automation and Programmability keeps its 10% but is folded in with AI and operations.

Here are both blueprints as Cisco publishes them, with our reading of how the old domains land in the new structure. The left two columns are Cisco’s. The right column is ours.

Two things are worth pulling out of that table. First, half the exam is now infrastructure and switching, up from 40%. If you are weak on VLANs, trunking and spanning tree, v2.0 punishes that harder than v1.1 does. Second, the combined weight of services and security drops from 25% to 20% — a small loss, and the only domain that genuinely shrinks.

Third, and this is the one that surprises people: the AI content did not arrive by pushing something out. It moved in with automation, and the pair of them still total 10%. If you were already comfortable with the v1.1 automation domain, you are looking at a sliver of new material inside a domain you have met, not a new frontier. Our CCNA 200-301 syllabus guide covers the current six domains in the depth this comparison does not have room for.

What does the new AI content in CCNA v2.0 actually cover?

Cisco names AI as one of the six areas the v2.0 exam covers, and it sits inside the 10% domain shared with network operations and management. INE’s July 2026 review of the blueprint reports objectives on generative AI, agentic AI and choosing a prompt for network operations work, specifying components such as persona, instructions and output format.

That is a narrower thing than the headlines suggest. Nobody is asking a CCNA candidate to train a model. The objectives INE describes are about using AI tooling inside an operations workflow — knowing what a generative system can and cannot be trusted with, and being able to frame a request to one properly.

It is also, if you think about it, a fair reflection of the job. A network engineer in 2027 will have an AI assistant somewhere in the loop, suggesting a configuration or summarising a log. The skill being tested is whether you can tell a good suggestion from a confidently wrong one, which requires exactly the fundamentals the other 90% of the exam covers.

AI enters the CCNA inside the same 10% domain that used to hold automation and programmability on its own.

Why is troubleshooting the real change in CCNA v2.0?

Cisco’s own description of the refresh emphasises practical assessment and more troubleshooting, and the question types still include performance-based items alongside multiple choice and drag-and-drop. Community analyses since the May 2026 announcement put troubleshooting near 28% of the v2.0 blueprint. Cisco itself publishes no troubleshooting percentage, so treat that number as community arithmetic.

Whatever the exact figure, the direction is not in doubt, and it matters more than the domain reshuffle. A recall question asks what OSPF does. A troubleshooting question gives you a neighbour relationship that will not form and four plausible reasons why. The second kind cannot be revised from a question bank, and it is the kind an interviewer asks too.

This is also why we are relaxed about telling students to carry on with v1.1. Lab fluency is the thing both versions reward, and the hours you put on real kit now are worth the same in March 2027 as in January. We made that argument in detail in Packet Tracer vs a real Cisco lab, and the v2.0 refresh strengthens it rather than weakening it.

What has been removed from the CCNA blueprint?

Very little is genuinely gone; what changed is what Cisco spells out. INE reports that the v2.0 blueprint no longer explicitly lists IPv6 address types or MAC learning and frame flooding, while still testing IPv6 troubleshooting, IPv6 prefix sizing and OSPFv3. An item dropping off the printed list does not stop an examiner asking about it.

This is the single most misread part of the refresh. A blueprint is a statement of what will be assessed, not an exhaustive index of every fact involved. You cannot troubleshoot an IPv6 address plan without knowing what a link-local address is, and you cannot diagnose a Layer 2 loop without knowing how a switch learns MAC addresses. Those topics left the list; they did not leave the exam.

So if you are mid-way through a v1.1 study plan, do not go through your notes deleting things. The practical risk of over-preparing on a removed topic is a few wasted hours. The practical risk of skipping a foundation because a list no longer names it is failing a troubleshooting question you should have got.

Will the CCNA v2.0 exam be harder than v1.1?

Nobody can answer that honestly yet, because no candidate has sat CCNA v2.0 and Cisco publishes no pass rates for either version. What is knowable: the exam stays 120 minutes while leaning further into scenario and troubleshooting questions, which take longer to read and answer than recall questions do.

Hold those two facts together and you get the only defensible prediction. Same clock, heavier questions. That is a time-management problem rather than a knowledge problem, and time management is exactly what lab hours fix — you stop reasoning out what a command does and start recognising it.

Be sceptical of anyone selling you a difficulty figure. We have seen pages quote v2.0 pass rates for an exam nobody has taken. If a claim about v2.0 is not traceable to Cisco’s exam topics page, Cisco’s learning blog or the official PDF, it is somebody’s opinion with a number attached.

Should you sit CCNA v1.1 now or wait for CCNA v2.0?

For most people studying in October 2026 the answer is to sit v1.1, because both versions award exactly the same CCNA credential. When does waiting for CCNA v2.0 actually make sense?

Waiting makes sense in three specific situations: you are starting from zero with under ten study hours a week, your employer will only fund one attempt in 2027, or you want the AI and troubleshooting content in your study plan for a specific role. Otherwise the calendar is against you.

Look closely at what those three have in common. None of them is “v2.0 is better”. They are all situations where the sixteen weeks do not fit, or where somebody else controls the booking. That is a scheduling decision and a perfectly rational one.

Sit v1.1 if you have been studying for a few months, you can get a clear run at it before late January, or you want a certificate on your CV for this hiring season rather than the next one.
Sit v1.1 if you are already booked. There is no benefit to cancelling an appointment that falls on or before 2 February 2027.
Plan for v2.0 if you are starting the fundamentals in November or December 2026 with a full-time job and under ten honest hours a week.
Plan for v2.0 if your company reimburses one attempt per year and will not approve a booking before February.
Do not wait because you heard v1.1 will look outdated. It will not. The credential is identical and no advert names a version.
Do not wait to avoid the troubleshooting emphasis. It is coming to every Cisco exam you sit after this one, and to every interview.

Do Hyderabad employers care which CCNA version you hold?

Not one listing on the first page of Naukri’s Hyderabad and Secunderabad CCNA results names an exam version. We read 658 CCNA vacancies there on 10 October 2026, 56 of them open to zero years of experience and 291 at three years. The strings 200-301, v1.1 and v2.0 appear nowhere on that page.

That is the answer to the question students are really asking, and it is worth stating plainly because the version debate online never mentions it. Employers filter on the credential name, the years of experience and the technologies. Not the blueprint edition.

How should you prepare for CCNA v2.0 from Hyderabad?

Build the hands first, because troubleshooting and performance-based questions cannot be revised from a question bank. Both blueprints share the same core: addressing, switching, routing, services and security. Time on real routers, switches, firewalls and wireless controllers transfers to either version, and to the interview that follows it.

Practically, that means your study plan should not change much at all. Work the fundamentals in the order the current blueprint teaches them, spend more of your week consoled into kit than reading, and treat the AI objectives as a short final block rather than a reason to restart. If you are training at a CCNA and CCNP networking course in Hyderabad, the question to ask your institute is not “are you v2.0 ready” — it is how many of your hours are on physical devices.

1
Read the official PDF, not a summary. Download Cisco’s v2.0 exam topics PDF from the exam topics page and keep it open beside whatever course you buy.
2
Fix switching before anything else. Infrastructure and switching together are half of v2.0, up from 40% in v1.1. VLANs, trunks, spanning tree, EtherChannel.
3
Practise diagnosis, not recall. Break your own lab deliberately: shut an interface, mismatch a native VLAN, fat-finger a subnet mask, then find it under time pressure.
4
Decide your exam date in writing. On or before 2 February 2027 means v1.1. After it means v2.0. Pick one and book backwards from it.
5
Leave the AI block last. It is a sliver of a 10% domain. Learn it properly, but do not let it displace the 90% that decides whether you pass.

Does a CCNA earned on v1.1 stay valid after February 2027?

A CCNA passed on v1.1 is a CCNA, carrying the same three-year validity Cisco applies to associate certifications, and Cisco does not label certificates by blueprint version. Cisco’s refresh page says the skills built for v1.1 carry straight into v2.0. Recertification rules are unchanged; the v2.0 exam itself counts toward them.

This is the fear underneath most “should I wait” questions, and it is unfounded. A certificate earned on 1 February 2027 and one earned on 4 February 2027 say the same words and are worth the same thing to the same employer. The only asymmetry is that the second person studied a slightly different list.
