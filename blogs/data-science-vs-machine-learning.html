Ask Google this question from India and the page it puts first is 420 words long. Ask it again and look at what Google itself volunteers in the People Also Ask box, and the top question is "Who earns more, ML engineer or data scientist?" — answered, on an India-localised search, entirely in US dollars.

That is the state of this keyword on 16 October 2026. Eight pages explaining that data science is broad and machine learning is narrow. Not one of them checking either claim against a single Indian job advert or a single Indian salary figure.

So this guide does the comparison the other way round. Every number below came from naukri.com for Hyderabad and Secunderabad, and from PayScale India, read on 16 October 2026. Two of them are worth knowing before you read any further: the two job titles were advertised 3,875 and 3,741 times in the same city, and their average base pay is 0.5% apart.

What is the difference between data science and machine learning?

Data science is the whole job of turning raw data into a decision, and machine learning is one set of techniques used inside that job to make predictions. Every machine learning project is a data science project. Most data science work advertised in Hyderabad never reaches a model: it stops at cleaning, querying and reporting.

That is the textbook answer and it is correct. It is also almost useless if what you are really asking is which course to buy and which job to chase, which is what most people typing this phrase want to know. The rest of this page answers that version instead, using the six questions Google's own People Also Ask box shows for this query.

Why is Google answering this question in US dollars?

Google's People Also Ask box for this query answers "who earns more" with United States medians, roughly $165,000 for a machine learning engineer against $158,000 for a data scientist, even on an India-localised search. That 15 to 40 percent premium is a real American pattern. Indian salary data does not reproduce it.

The sources it pulls from are a Reddit thread, Coursera, 365 Data Science, a Medium post and a Quora answer. All competent, all written about the American market. Nothing in that block is wrong; it is simply answering a different country's question, and it is the figure most Indian readers will see first and remember.

Does machine learning fall under data science?

Machine learning sits inside artificial intelligence as a field of study, and inside data science as a stage of work, which is why the two answers you find online contradict each other. GeeksforGeeks, ranking first for this query, calls machine learning a subset of artificial intelligence in one paragraph and a subset of data science in another.

Both statements are defensible, because they answer different questions. Where does the subject belong academically? Under artificial intelligence. Where does it belong on a Monday morning? In the middle of a data science workflow, after someone has already found, cleaned and understood the data. If you want that workflow laid out end to end first, our beginner's guide to what data science actually is walks through it stage by stage.

Who earns more in India, a machine learning engineer or a data scientist?

In India the two are effectively level: PayScale reports an average base of Rs 10,22,861 for a machine learning engineer and Rs 10,17,652 for a data scientist, a gap of Rs 5,209 or about half a percent. At one to four years, the data scientist average is actually higher.

This is the opposite of the American pattern Google is serving. On PayScale India, early-career data scientists at one to four years average Rs 10,05,147 against Rs 8,40,657 for machine learning engineers at the same stage, which puts the data scientist roughly 19 percent ahead. Entry level is a dead heat: Rs 5,95,255 against Rs 5,96,082, a difference of Rs 827 a year.

Which is better, machine learning or data science?

Neither is better in Hyderabad in 2026, because the advert volumes, the salary bands and the education filters are almost identical; what differs is the kind of work and the shape of the ceiling. Machine learning engineering pays a higher top end, Rs 30 lakh against Rs 20 lakh at the ninetieth percentile.

So the honest framing is not better or worse but wider or taller. Data science is the wider door at one to four years and the better-evidenced average. Machine learning engineering is the taller ladder once you can ship and maintain software. Most people in this city arrive at the second through the first, which is why the shared foundation matters more than the choice of label.

What does each role actually do day to day?

A data scientist spends most of the week on SQL, cleaning, exploratory analysis and explaining a result to a business owner; a machine learning engineer spends most of it on Python services, pipelines, deployment and monitoring a model that is already live. Both write code. Only one is judged on uptime.

The clearest tell is what breaks your week. For a data scientist it is a number that does not reconcile, or a stakeholder who does not believe the result. For a machine learning engineer it is a prediction endpoint timing out at two in the morning. If being on the hook for a running system sounds like a burden rather than a craft, that is a real answer about which side you belong on.

Which skills overlap, and which do not?

Python, SQL, statistics and the core model families are shared ground, so roughly the first half of any serious curriculum serves both roles. The split starts after the model works: a data scientist adds visualisation and communication, while a machine learning engineer adds Docker, cloud deployment, APIs and monitoring.

This is the practical reason we do not run two separate beginner tracks. The twelve-block Data Science programme at our KPHB 5th Phase campus teaches the shared half properly — Python plus SQL, Stats, Data Visualization, then Supervised and unSupervised Learning — before anyone has to decide which door they are walking through. The Hyderabad adverts justify that order: both titles screen on the same foundation.

What each side adds after the shared foundation

Data science adds — experiment design, a visualisation tool, stakeholder communication, and the discipline of writing down what a result does not prove.
Machine learning engineering adds — version control discipline, containers, cloud services, API design, retraining schedules and drift monitoring.
Both now add generative AI — Hyderabad adverts for both titles name large language models, retrieval augmented generation, LangChain and function calling.

Which course is better, an AIML course or a data science course?

For a beginner in Hyderabad a data science course is the safer first purchase, because its Python, SQL and statistics foundation is the entry requirement for both tracks, while an AIML-only syllabus assumes you already have it. Check the module list before you pay, not the course name.

Course names are marketing; module lists are the product. If a syllabus labelled AIML opens with neural networks and never mentions SQL, it is a second course, not a first one. If you are specifically after the large language model and agent side of the work, that is what the Generative AI and Agentic AI programme in Kukatpally is built for, and it expects the Python foundation on day one.

Will AI replace data scientists?

Generative AI is changing what the job asks for rather than removing it: Hyderabad listings now routinely name large language models, retrieval augmented generation and LangChain inside data scientist adverts. Infosys advertised a Hyderabad data scientist role naming GenAI, LLM and RAG in its title, at two to five years.

Read the adverts rather than the headlines. In the same Hyderabad search on 16 October 2026, EY tagged a data scientist role at one to six years with agentic AI, LangGraph, retrieval augmented generation, function calling, Semantic Kernel and LangChain. Fractal Analytics advertised "Data Scientist, Gen AI" at one to six years. The title survived; the required toolkit moved. What is genuinely at risk is the narrow version of the job that only produced charts.
