Packet Tracer vs Real Cisco Lab: What Employers Actually Test We read every network engineer advert in Hyderabad on one day and checked the kit against what Packet Tracer can actually show you

This argument is usually fought on learning theory — is a simulator realistic enough, does the knowledge stick, should you buy second-hand routers. We went at it from the other end. On 8 October 2026 we read the Hyderabad and Secunderabad network engineer listings on Naukri: 1,624 of them. Then we took the hardware and software those adverts name and checked it against Cisco Packet Tracer’s own device list. Palo Alto, Fortinet, Arista, F5, Aruba, Catalyst Center, SD-WAN, Juniper Mist. None of it is in Packet Tracer. Not a simplified version of it — it is not there. That changes the question from “is the simulator realistic” to “can the simulator show me the equipment in the job advert”, and the honest answer is mostly no.

A student came to the KPHB counter last month with a laptop full of Packet Tracer files. Twenty-odd topologies, all neat, all working. He had passed every practice test he could find. He had also just been rejected after a technical round where the interviewer asked him to describe what he does when a switch comes up with no configuration and no network access, and he had no answer, because in Packet Tracer a switch is simply there.

That is the real shape of this question. Not whether Packet Tracer is good software — it is, and it is free, and you should use it. The question is what it leaves out, and whether what it leaves out is the part employers are hiring for.

So instead of arguing about realism, we went and read the job market. On 8 October 2026 we pulled the network engineer listings for Hyderabad and Secunderabad on Naukri and went through what they ask for. Then we checked that list against Packet Tracer’s own device inventory. The gap is wider than the usual debate admits, and it is not really about realism at all.

Is Packet Tracer enough, or do you need a real Cisco lab?

Cisco Packet Tracer is enough to understand CCNA 200-301 topics and to pass the exam, but it is not enough to interview like someone who has worked on networks. It omits real Cisco IOS, nearly all debug output, most show commands, and every non-Cisco device — and non-Cisco kit appears in most Hyderabad job adverts.

Both halves of that matter. If you are short of money and time, Packet Tracer plus a good book will get you certified, and plenty of working engineers started exactly there. But a certificate is a filter, not the interview. The interview is where somebody asks what you did when the cable was in the wrong port.

What is Cisco Packet Tracer, and what is it actually for?

Packet Tracer is Cisco’s free network simulation tool, built so learners can design and configure topologies in a virtual lab with, in Cisco’s own words on its product page, “no hardware needed”. It was written as a teaching aid for the Networking Academy curriculum, and that is the job it does well.

Read that description again, because the word doing the work is simulation. A simulator does not run the software a real device runs. It reproduces the behaviour its authors chose to reproduce, in the way they chose to reproduce it. When you type a command into Packet Tracer, you are not talking to Cisco IOS. You are talking to a program that has been written to respond the way IOS responds — for the commands somebody implemented.

That is a design decision, not a flaw. It is why Packet Tracer runs on a four-year-old laptop, installs in two minutes and costs nothing, while an emulator needs real operating-system images and a machine with memory to spare. For learning how a VLAN works, how a routing table is built, how a trunk carries tagged frames, it is excellent. It is the best free tool in networking education and the reason thousands of people in Hyderabad can start studying tonight.

The trouble starts when a simulator is the only thing somebody has ever touched, and nobody has told them what the boundaries are.

What can Packet Tracer not do that real Cisco equipment can?

The core limitation is that Packet Tracer does not run genuine Cisco IOS, so its features are re-implemented approximations. Among first-hop redundancy protocols it supports HSRP but not VRRP or GLBP, its show commands are heavily reduced, debugging is very limited, and release 9.0 ships Cisco devices only.

Those specifics come from a review by a working engineer at howtonetwork.com, published 17 February 2022 and last updated 12 May 2026, which is the most honest account of the boundaries we found. Here is the practical version of that list, with what each gap costs you on the job.

What Packet Tracer does not give youWhy it matters at work
Real Cisco IOS — behaviour is re-implemented, not genuineCommands that work in the simulator can be rejected on real kit, and syntax you never learned exists
Full show command set — very limited selectionshow output is how engineers diagnose. Reading it fluently is the skill being tested
Meaningful debug — heavily restrictedNAT and WAN faults are usually found in debug output. You cannot practise what you cannot run
VRRP and GLBP — only HSRP among FHRPsMulti-vendor sites run VRRP. An advert naming it is an advert you cannot speak to
Any non-Cisco device — Cisco catalogue onlyCovered in detail below; this is the biggest gap and the least discussed

That last row is the one no software fixes. In Packet Tracer you drag a cable and it connects. On real equipment you work out which of two identical-looking ports is the right one, find a console cable and a working serial adapter, set the terminal to 9600 baud, and watch a boot sequence that sometimes stops where you did not expect. Seating an interface module and cabling fibre properly are physical skills. So is realising a device is fine and the patch cable is dead.

Packet Tracer is still worth your time

Nothing here is an argument against using it. Packet Tracer is free, it runs anywhere, and it is the fastest way to build and rebuild a topology twenty times until the configuration order makes sense in your head. Use it heavily. Just do not mistake it for experience, and do not let it be the only thing on your CV under “hands-on”.

What does the Hyderabad network engineer job market actually ask for?

On 8 October 2026 Naukri listed 1,624 network engineer vacancies in Hyderabad and Secunderabad, of which 1,464 required attendance at an office and only 22 were fully remote. That is 90.1% on-site work. Just 89 listings, or 5.5%, were open to candidates with zero years of experience.

We are quoting the portal’s own filter counts, read the same morning. Keyword matching on a job board is approximate and the numbers move daily, but the shape of the market is clear enough to plan around.

Which technologies in those job adverts do not exist in Packet Tracer?

Most of them. Listings read that day named Palo Alto next-generation firewalls, Fortinet, Arista, F5 load balancing, Aruba wireless, Cisco Catalyst Center, SD-WAN and Juniper Mist. Packet Tracer 9.0 ships Cisco devices only, and its single firewall is the Cisco ISA-3000. None of that equipment is in it.

Packet Tracer vs GNS3 vs EVE-NG vs Cisco Modeling Labs: which does what?

Packet Tracer simulates devices, while GNS3, EVE-NG and Cisco Modeling Labs emulate them by running real network operating system images. Emulation means commands behave genuinely, which closes the IOS gap but not the physical one. Cisco’s own learning community describes Packet Tracer as a small simulator that cannot pass real traffic.

The distinction between simulate and emulate is the one worth carrying away. A simulator imitates behaviour; an emulator runs the actual software. Here is the honest positioning of the four.

ToolTypeBest forMain catch
Packet TracerSimulatorCCNA 200-301 fundamentals, free, runs on any laptopNot real IOS; Cisco catalogue only; limited show and debug
GNS3EmulatorReal IOS behaviour, multi-vendor topologiesYou must supply licensed images legally; setup is fiddly
EVE-NGEmulatorLarge multi-vendor labs, CCNP-level studySame image-licensing problem; needs real RAM and CPU
Cisco Modeling LabsEmulator, officialGenuine Cisco images with Cisco support, CCNP and CCIELicensed product; heavier on resources than Packet Tracer
Physical labReal equipmentCabling, console, modules, power, boot, multi-vendor kitCosts money and space; this is what a training lab is for

Notice that moving up that table fixes the software gap and never touches the hardware gap. An emulator will teach you correct syntax and real protocol behaviour. It will not teach you that the console cable is in the auxiliary port. Do employers accept Packet Tracer experience in an interview?

Employers accept Packet Tracer as evidence that you studied, not as evidence that you have worked. Interviewers in Hyderabad tend to find the boundary with one or two questions about physical behaviour — console access, boot sequence, a fault that turned out to be cabling — because those answers cannot be learned from software.

You do not need to hide the simulator. Say it plainly: you built your foundation in Packet Tracer and then moved onto physical equipment. That is a normal, respectable path and it is how most people learn. What costs you the round is claiming hands-on experience and then not knowing what a rack looks like.

The questions that separate simulator-only candidates

“A switch arrives with no configuration. How do you get into it?” — the console cable answer. In a simulator you click the device and a CLI appears.
“Walk me through what you see on boot.” — you either have watched a real boot sequence or you have not, and it shows in about five seconds.
“The config is correct and the link is still down. What now?” — cabling, port, module, duplex, SFP. Simulators rarely give you a bad cable.
“Have you written a firewall policy?” — a quarter of the adverts we read name Palo Alto or Fortinet. Neither exists in Packet Tracer.
Do not say “I have two years of hands-on experience” when you mean two years of simulator practice. One follow-up question ends the conversation badly.

We put these in front of students in the mock interview sessions that are Module 6 of the programme, and the pattern is consistent: the people who struggle are not the ones with weaker theory. They are the ones who have never been in a room with the equipment. Our post on CCNA interview questions for freshers goes through the technical side of the round in detail.

How should you split your study time between simulator and hardware?

Roughly 80% simulator and 20% real equipment is the split a working engineer recommends, and it holds up well. Use Packet Tracer for repetition — building the same topology until the configuration order is automatic — and use physical equipment for everything the simulator smooths over, which is where interview answers come from.

That 80/20 figure comes from the howtonetwork.com review cited above. The reasoning is sound: repetition is cheap in software and expensive on hardware, so do your volume in the simulator. But the 20% is not optional, and it is not interchangeable with more simulator hours.

A workable study order

Weeks 1–4, Packet Tracer only. Learn addressing, VLANs, trunking, static and dynamic routing. Build each topology from an empty canvas, not from a saved file.
From week 4, add hardware. Console in. Watch a boot. Break something deliberately and fix it. One session a week beside a rack changes how you read output.
Add an emulator if you are going past CCNA. GNS3, EVE-NG or Cisco Modeling Labs for real IOS behaviour, VRRP, and protocols Packet Tracer handles only partly.
Then the multi-vendor kit. Firewall policy on Palo Alto or Fortigate, and an access point joined to a wireless LAN controller. These are the differentiators in the adverts.
Finish with mock interviews. Theory is not what fails people. Being asked about a rack you have never stood next to is.

If you have no access to equipment at all, be strategic rather than discouraged. Do the simulator work properly, be honest about what it is, and find hardware time somewhere — an institute lab, a workplace rack, or second-hand gear if your budget stretches.
