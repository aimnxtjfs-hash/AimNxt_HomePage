Search for VAPT interview questions and you get lists. Fifty questions, a hundred questions, answers a sentence long, all in one flat pile. They are not wrong. They are just not how an interview happens.

An interview happens in rounds. Somebody on a phone call decides in eleven minutes whether to pass you to the technical panel. The panel decides whether you have method or vocabulary. Then somebody hands you a real artefact and watches you think. Each round is testing something different, and a question that would be a gentle opener in round one is a trap in round two, because the follow-up changes.

We read the whole first page of Google for this query from India on 25 September 2026. No AI Overview. No People Also Ask box. The depth leader is a 4,200-word list of fifty questions published on 8 September 2026 — good material, genuinely. But not one page on that first result page tells you which round a question belongs to, what the interviewer asks next, or that the OWASP answer most candidates have memorised is now describing a list that is no longer current.

So that is what this is. Twenty-five questions, sorted by round, with the answer and the follow-up. Plus the Hyderabad numbers, because they explain why the screening round is as brutal as it is.

What Questions Are Asked in a VAPT Interview?

A VAPT interview opens with eight fundamentals: vulnerability assessment versus penetration testing, the phases of an engagement, threat versus vulnerability versus risk, black box versus grey box, your toolset, the OWASP Top 10, what CVSS measures, and authorisation. Technical depth on injection, Active Directory and APIs comes only after those eight are cleared.

Hold that order in your head, because it is the reason so many technically capable freshers never reach a technical panel. They prepared Kerberoasting and lost the call on question one.

The single test that predicts the outcome

For every answer you have prepared, ask yourself: can I give one specific example from something I actually did? If the answer is a definition with no example behind it, you have memorised, not learned — and the follow-up question will find that out in about ten seconds.

How Is a VAPT Interview Actually Structured?

Most Indian VAPT hiring loops run four rounds: a screening call on fundamentals, a technical round on depth, a practical task in a lab or on a Burp request, and a judgement round on communication and ethics. Freshers are eliminated in round one far more often than in round three.

Smaller consultancies compress rounds two and three into one ninety-minute session. Large service companies sometimes add a managerial round that is really a culture and availability conversation. The shape holds either way, and it is worth knowing which round you are in when you sit down, because it tells you how much detail to give.

RoundWhat it is really testingHow long
1. ScreeningDo you know the vocabulary, and can you use it in a sentence? Eight definition questions, asked fast.10–20 min
2. TechnicalMethod over terminology. How you found something, not what it is called.45–60 min
3. PracticalPrioritisation and narration on a real artefact — a request, a scan export, a shell.30–60 min
4. JudgementWhether you can be trusted with client systems and client conversations.20–30 min

Our own composite of the loop Indian employers and consultancies most commonly run for VAPT and application-security roles. Individual companies vary — some skip round three entirely for freshers, and a few replace it with a take-home assignment. Treat this as a map, not a promise about any specific employer.

Which Questions Come Up in the Screening Round?

The screening round is eight definition questions, and the first one is almost always the difference between a vulnerability assessment and a penetration test. A vulnerability assessment lists weaknesses broadly with scanners; a penetration test proves exploitability narrowly by hand. Indian clients buy both together, which is why the acronym VAPT exists at all.

Answer that one in two sentences and then add a third the interviewer did not ask for: which of the two a specific client needs first. A company with four hundred servers and no security team needs the assessment. A company that has already fixed the obvious things and wants to know whether an attacker could actually chain them needs the test. That third sentence is what separates a candidate who read a definition from one who understands why a client pays.

The phases question comes next, and both common answers are correct as long as you name your framework. The Penetration Testing Execution Standard defines seven stages, while NIST’s Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment sets out a four-phase model of planning, discovery, attack and reporting. Name which one you are using. Candidates who mix stages from both frameworks into one improvised list get caught immediately. If you want the full picture of what those phases involve in practice, our explainer on what VAPT actually is walks through them.

The eight screening questions, in the order they usually arrive

Vulnerability assessment versus penetration testing. Breadth and automation against depth and manual proof — then say which a given client needs first.
The phases of an engagement. Seven PTES stages or four NIST phases. Name the framework before you list anything.
Threat, vulnerability, risk. Three distinct things. Give a banking example rather than three definitions.
Black box, grey box, white box. How much you are told up front, and what each buys the client for the same budget.
Your toolset. Six tools you have genuinely used, and what each is for. Never list a tool you cannot be questioned on.
The OWASP Top 10. Quote the 2025 edition and say the year. See the next section — this one has changed.
What CVSS measures. Technical severity, not business risk. Be ready for the CVSS 9.8-on-a-lab-box trap.
Authorisation. Written scope, rules of engagement, dates, exclusions. Say it before anyone asks you to.

What Is the Right Answer to the OWASP Top 10 Question in 2026?

Quote the OWASP Top 10:2025, name the year out loud, and lead with A01 Broken Access Control and A02 Security Misconfiguration. Two categories are new in that edition: A03 Software Supply Chain Failures and A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions. Reciting the 2021 list in 2026 tells the interviewer where you studied.

This is the one genuinely dated answer in almost every VAPT interview guide currently ranking, including the good ones. They were written against the 2021 list, and the categories have moved since. We read the current list directly from the OWASP Top 10:2025 project page on 25 September 2026.

#OWASP Top 10:2025 categoryWorth knowing in an interview
A01Broken Access ControlStill first. IDOR and privilege-escalation findings live here.
A02Security MisconfigurationMoved up. The category most freshers under-rate and most scanners over-report.
A03Software Supply Chain FailuresNew. Broader than vulnerable dependencies — build systems and pipelines count.
A04Cryptographic FailuresWeak algorithms, bad key handling, data in transit and at rest.
A05InjectionSQL, command and template injection, with cross-site scripting folded in.
A06Insecure DesignFlaws no scanner reports. Where business-logic findings sit.
A07Authentication FailuresCredential stuffing, session handling, multi-factor bypass.
A08Software or Data Integrity FailuresUnverified updates, insecure deserialisation, tampered artefacts.
A09Security Logging and Alerting FailuresRenamed. The gap between an attack happening and anyone noticing.
A10Mishandling of Exceptional ConditionsNew. Error handling and failure states as a security category in their own right.

Category names and order read directly from top10.owasp.org/2025 on 25 September 2026. The right-hand column is our own interview commentary, not part of the OWASP publication. If an interviewer asks for the 2021 list specifically, give it — but say which edition you are quoting either way, because that habit alone marks you as someone who checks his sources.

There is a bonus in this. Being the candidate who says “the 2025 edition moved Security Misconfiguration to A02 and added Software Supply Chain Failures” does more for you than any amount of depth on a single vulnerability, because it demonstrates that you follow the field rather than a course handout from three years ago.

Which Technical Questions Separate Freshers From Hires?

The technical round rewards method over vocabulary: how you test for SQL injection when SQLMap finds nothing, why DOM-based cross-site scripting gets missed, and what a successful Kerberoasting attack actually hands you. Twelve of the twenty-five questions below sit in this round, and every one has a follow-up asking how, not what.

The pattern is consistent enough to prepare for. The panel asks a what question, you answer it, and then they ask a how question about the same thing. “What is SSRF?” is followed by “how would you test for it on an endpoint that only accepts internal hostnames?” A memorised definition survives the first question and dies on the second.

So prepare in pairs. For every concept, know the definition and one thing you have actually done with it — a request you modified, a flag you passed, a response that told you something. Even a lab box counts, as long as you did it yourself and can describe it in order. Candidates with three well-understood findings out-perform candidates with thirty half-remembered concepts, every time.

Injection, tested by hand

Expect to be asked how you test for SQL injection without a tool: a single quote, a boolean pair that should return different pages, a time-delay payload, then error text. Naming SQLMap first is the wrong opening.

Cross-site scripting, in context

Three types, and the one they care about is DOM-based, because it never touches the server and so never appears in server-side testing. Context decides the payload: HTML body, attribute, JavaScript string, URL.

Active Directory, one chain

You do not need every attack. You need one full chain you can narrate: enumerate, find a service account, Kerberoast it, crack the hash offline, move sideways. Naming attacks without a chain reads as flashcards.

APIs, where the bugs moved

Broken object-level authorisation is the API question that comes up most, because it is the flaw scanners miss and reviewers find. Know why an API test is mostly authorisation work rather than input work.

What Happens in the Practical or Lab Round?

The practical round hands you something real: a captured Burp Suite request, a Nessus export with 400 findings, or a shell on a domain-joined Windows box, and asks what you would do next. Nobody expects a finished exploit in fifteen minutes. They are watching how you prioritise and narrate.

Narration is the whole skill here, and it is the one nobody practises. Say what you are looking at, say what you suspect, say what you would test to confirm or kill that suspicion, then do it. A candidate who thinks silently for four minutes and produces the right answer scores lower than one who thinks out loud and produces a reasonable wrong answer, because the panel is hiring the process.

The four-hundred-findings question deserves special preparation because it is really a question about judgement. Nobody wants to hear that you would triage all four hundred. They want to hear that you would first strip out the informational noise, then group by asset criticality, then manually validate the handful that are externally reachable and actually exploitable, and tell the client which five to fix this week. Our guide to writing a penetration testing report covers how that triage then turns into a document.

Which Judgement Questions Do Candidates Fail Most?

Two judgement questions end most VAPT interviews: explain a vulnerability to a non-technical executive, and describe what you did when a test caused an outage. Both test whether you can be trusted with client access. Technically strong candidates lose offers here more often than on any Active Directory question.

For the first, drop every acronym. A finance director does not need to know what a parameter is; he needs to know that a customer could read another customer’s invoices by changing a number in the address bar, that it takes no special tools, and what it would cost if a regulator found it first. Impact, effort, consequence. Sixty seconds.

For the second, never claim it has never happened. It happens to everyone who tests real systems. Answer with the sequence: stopped testing immediately, called the named contact from the rules-of-engagement document, described exactly what I had run and when, helped restore, documented it in the report with timestamps. That answer is worth more than a clean record, because the panel is not asking whether you break things. They are asking what you do in the ten minutes afterwards.

Showing 25 of 25 questions

1.What is the difference between a vulnerability assessment and a penetration test?

ScreeningFresher

A vulnerability assessment covers a wide estate with scanners and produces a prioritised list of weaknesses without proving any of them are exploitable. A penetration test goes narrow and deep, chaining flaws manually to demonstrate real impact on a specific target.

Follow-up: “A client has 400 servers and no security team. Which do they need first, and why?”

2.Walk me through the phases of a penetration test.

ScreeningFresher

Name your framework first. The Penetration Testing Execution Standard has seven stages: pre-engagement, intelligence gathering, threat modelling, vulnerability analysis, exploitation, post-exploitation, reporting. NIST SP 800-115 uses four: planning, discovery, attack, reporting.

Follow-up: “Which phase do you spend the most time in, and which one do most testers rush?”

3.What is the difference between a threat, a vulnerability and a risk?

ScreeningFresher

A vulnerability is the weakness, a threat is the actor or event that could use it, and risk is the combination of likelihood and business impact. Answer with one worked example rather than three definitions.

Follow-up: “Give me all three for an unpatched internet-facing file server at a bank.”

4.What are black box, grey box and white box testing?

ScreeningFresher

Black box means no prior information, grey box means partial access such as a low-privilege login, and white box means source code and architecture. Grey box usually gives a client the most findings per rupee spent.

Follow-up: “If the budget only covers one, which do you recommend and what do you tell the client they are losing?”

5.Which tools do you use, and what do you use each one for?

ScreeningFresher

Name six you have genuinely used and pair each with a purpose: Nmap for discovery, Burp Suite for web traffic, Nessus or OpenVAS for scanning, SQLMap for confirming injection, Metasploit for exploitation, Gobuster for content discovery.

Follow-up: “What does Nmap’s -sS do differently from -sT, and when does that difference matter?”

6.What is the OWASP Top 10, and which edition are you quoting?

ScreeningFresher

It is a consensus list of the most critical web application security risks. Quote the 2025 edition and say the year: A01 Broken Access Control, A02 Security Misconfiguration, A03 Software Supply Chain Failures, then the rest.

Follow-up: “Name two categories that are new or renamed in the 2025 edition compared with 2021.”

7.What does CVSS measure, and what does it not measure?

ScreeningFresher

CVSS scores the technical characteristics of a vulnerability on a zero-to-ten scale. It does not know what the asset is worth, who can reach it, or what compensating controls exist, so it is a starting point for severity, not the final answer.

Follow-up: “A finding scores 9.8 on an isolated lab box with no data. Is it critical? Defend your answer.”

8.What do you need in writing before you start testing?

ScreeningFresher

Signed authorisation naming the in-scope assets, the test window, the rules of engagement, explicit exclusions, an escalation contact, and confirmation that the asset owner is the one authorising it. Testing outside that document is not a pentest.

Follow-up: “Mid-test you find evidence of an existing live breach. What do you do, in order?”

9.Explain the TCP three-way handshake and how a SYN scan abuses it.

TechnicalFresher

SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK establishes a connection. A SYN scan sends the first packet, reads the reply to infer the port state, and never completes the handshake, so it is faster and historically less likely to be logged by the application.

Follow-up: “Why does a SYN scan need root, and what changes if you only have an unprivileged shell?”

10.How would you enumerate subdomains for a target you know nothing about?

TechnicalFresher

Start passive: certificate transparency logs, public DNS datasets, and tools such as Amass and Sublist3r. Then go active with brute-force resolution and permutation wordlists, and finally resolve and screenshot everything to find what is actually alive.

Follow-up: “You find an unresolved subdomain pointing at a decommissioned cloud bucket. What is the finding?”

11.How do you test for SQL injection manually when SQLMap finds nothing?

TechnicalExperienced

Work by behaviour, not error text. Send a boolean pair that should return different responses, then a time-delay payload, then test every injection point the tool skipped: headers, cookies, JSON bodies, and second-order inputs that are stored and rendered elsewhere.

Follow-up: “Walk me through a second-order injection you have found, or explain exactly how you would hunt one.”

12.What are the three types of cross-site scripting, and why is DOM-based the one that gets missed?

TechnicalFresher

Reflected, stored and DOM-based. DOM-based XSS is missed because the payload is never processed server-side, so server-focused testing and most automated scanning never see it. You have to read the client-side JavaScript that writes into the page.

Follow-up: “Which sinks do you grep the JavaScript for first, and why does context change the payload?”

13.What is SSRF, and why is it usually rated higher than it first looks?

TechnicalExperienced

Server-side request forgery makes the server fetch a URL you control. It rates high because the server sits inside the network, so it reaches internal services, admin interfaces and cloud metadata endpoints that are unreachable from outside.

Follow-up: “The filter blocks anything that is not an internal hostname. How do you get around that?”

14.What is IDOR, and how is it different from broken access control?

TechnicalFresher

An insecure direct object reference is one specific pattern: a user-supplied identifier is used to fetch a record without an authorisation check. Broken access control is the parent category, A01 in the OWASP Top 10:2025, and covers far more than object references.

Follow-up: “How do you prove an IDOR without touching another customer’s real data?”

15.How do you test a file upload feature?

TechnicalFresher

Test what is validated and where. Extension, content type, magic bytes, size, and whether validation happens client-side only. Then find out where the file lands, whether it is served back, and whether the path is guessable or traversable.

Follow-up: “Upload succeeds but the file is stored outside the web root. Is there still a finding here?”

16.What is Kerberoasting, and what does a successful attack actually give you?

TechnicalExperienced

Any authenticated domain user can request a service ticket for an account with a service principal name. The ticket is encrypted with that account’s password hash, so you crack it offline. What you get is a service account password, not instant domain admin.

Follow-up: “The hash will not crack. What is your next move inside that domain?”

17.What do you check first for privilege escalation on Linux?

TechnicalExperienced

Cheap and reliable things first: sudo rights for the current user, SUID and SGID binaries, writable files and directories owned by root, scheduled jobs, and credentials sitting in configuration files or history. Kernel exploits come last because they crash hosts.

Follow-up: “Why is a kernel exploit the last option on a production server rather than the first?”

18.How does API testing differ from web application testing?

TechnicalExperienced

There is no interface to guide you, so you work from documentation, traffic captures and specification files. Most of the work is authorisation rather than input validation, because every endpoint is directly reachable without the flow a user interface would enforce.

Follow-up: “You have no documentation and no specification file. How do you map the API?”

19.What is broken object-level authorisation, and why is it the top API risk?

TechnicalExperienced

The endpoint authenticates the caller but never checks whether that caller owns the object being requested. It tops the API risk list because it is trivial to exploit, invisible to scanners that lack a second test account, and present in most APIs nobody has audited.

Follow-up: “How do you test for it systematically across sixty endpoints rather than one at a time?”

20.How do you test a JWT implementation?

TechnicalExperienced

Read the header and payload, then attack verification rather than content: algorithm set to none, algorithm confusion between asymmetric and symmetric, a weak or guessable signing secret, an unverified key identifier, and whether expiry and audience claims are actually checked server-side.

Follow-up: “The signature is verified properly. Is there anything left to test in that token?”

21.Here is a captured request. Find the flaw and tell me what you would try next.

PracticalExperienced

Narrate before you touch anything. Read the method, path, parameters, headers and any token, say what the endpoint appears to do, name the one parameter you find suspicious and why, then state the single test that would confirm or kill that suspicion.

Follow-up: “Your test returns a 403. What does that tell you, and what do you change?”

22.You have 400 scanner findings and two days. What do you do?

PracticalFresher

Strip informational and duplicate entries, group by asset criticality and external reachability, manually validate only the handful that are both reachable and genuinely exploitable, then hand the client five prioritised items with evidence rather than four hundred without it.

Follow-up: “The client insists every one of the 400 gets a line in the report. How do you handle that?”

23.You have a shell on a domain-joined Windows box. What are your first three commands?

PracticalExperienced

Establish context before doing anything loud: who you are and what privileges you hold, what the host is and what is running on it, and what the domain looks like from here. Enumeration first, escalation second, and note every command with a timestamp.

Follow-up: “You are already a local administrator. Does that change your next step at all?”

24.Explain SQL injection to a finance director in one minute.

JudgementFresher

No acronyms, no parameters. Say that the website passes whatever a visitor types straight to the database, so a visitor can ask the database for records that are not his, that it needs no special tools, and what it would cost if a regulator found it first.

Follow-up: “He asks whether he should shut the site down today. What do you say?”

25.Have you ever caused an outage during a test? What did you do?

JudgementFresher

Never claim it has never happened. Give the sequence: stopped testing, called the escalation contact named in the rules of engagement, described exactly what was run and when, helped restore service, then documented it in the report with timestamps.

Follow-up: “Who did you tell first, and what did you write in the report about it?”

What Makes a Candidate Fail a VAPT Interview?

Five failures repeat: reciting a memorised definition without an example, naming a tool without knowing what its flags do, quoting an outdated OWASP list, having no lab work to describe, and talking about exploits before authorisation. None of them are about intelligence. All five are fixable in a fortnight of deliberate practice.

The fifth one is the quiet killer. A candidate who launches into the attack he would run, without once mentioning scope or written permission, has told the panel something they cannot unhear — that he might do the same on a client network. Mention authorisation unprompted, early, once. It costs you eight seconds and it removes the only objection that ends an interview instantly.

1

Attach one example to every definition

Go through your prepared answers and write a single concrete example beside each. Not a textbook case — something you did, even on a lab box. If a definition has no example beside it after ten minutes of thinking, that is the gap in your preparation, not a gap in your notes.

2

Cut your tool list down to what you can defend

Six tools you have used beats twenty you have heard of. For each one, know the two or three flags or features you actually reach for and why. The follow-up is always about a flag, and “I have used it but I do not remember the options” is a worse answer than never naming the tool.

3

Rebuild three findings you can narrate end to end

Pick three vulnerabilities you have genuinely found, and practise telling each as a sequence: what I saw, what I suspected, what I tested, what confirmed it, what the impact was. Out loud, timed to ninety seconds. This single exercise carries you through both the technical and the practical round.

4

Rehearse the two judgement answers out loud

The executive explanation and the outage story. Write them, then say them to someone who does not work in security and watch where they lose you. These are the only two answers in the whole interview where being technically precise scores lower than being clearly understood.

How Do You Prepare for a VAPT Interview in 30 Days?

Spend the first ten days making the eight screening answers automatic, the next fifteen rebuilding three lab findings you can narrate end to end, and the last five rehearsing the two judgement questions out loud. Depth on one attack you actually performed beats shallow familiarity with twenty you read about.

Say your answers out loud, to a person or a recording. Reading an answer and speaking it are different skills, and the interview tests the second one. Most candidates discover on the call that an answer they knew perfectly on paper comes out as four disconnected fragments, which is a rehearsal problem rather than a knowledge problem.

If you would rather not assemble those three findings alone, that is what the four-month VAPT and Ethical Hacking training in Hyderabad exists to produce — fifteen modules of lab work and a capstone engagement you can narrate in any round.

In the last week, prepare your own three questions for them: what does a typical engagement look like here, who reviews reports, and what would I be doing in the first three months. Asking those makes the conversation two-way, and the answers tell you whether the role involves real testing or reading scanner output all day.

Frequently Asked Questions

A VAPT interview opens with eight or nine fundamentals: the difference between vulnerability assessment and penetration testing, the phases of an engagement, threat versus vulnerability versus risk, black box versus grey box, your tools, the OWASP Top 10, what CVSS measures, and how you handle authorisation. Technical depth follows only after those are cleared.
A vulnerability assessment finds and lists weaknesses across a wide estate, largely with automated scanners, and does not prove they are exploitable. A penetration test goes narrow and deep, chaining flaws manually to demonstrate real impact. Most Indian clients buy both together under the label VAPT, which is why the acronym exists.
Quote the OWASP Top 10:2025, the current edition, and say the year out loud. It leads with A01 Broken Access Control and A02 Security Misconfiguration, and adds two new categories: A03 Software Supply Chain Failures and A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions. Reciting the 2021 list in 2026 signals you learned from stale material.
